Architecture · read top to bottom

Annual Member Risk Model

On 1 January, predict each member's total allowed cost for the next 12 months from the previous 24 months of eligibility, medical, pharmacy and RAF data — then turn that dollar figure into a risk score (population average = 1.0) that beats CMS-HCC on the tests actuaries already use.

1
Raw data sources

Everything the plan already knows about a member, for the last 24 months

Nothing is modelled here. This stage only lands six feeds in one place with consistent member IDs and dates. The one subtlety is run-out: claims arrive late, so a "31 December" snapshot is actually pulled around March, or the missing tail is estimated.

IN
Source systems: enrollment, medical claims, pharmacy claims, CMS risk-adjustment output, optional lab/SDOH feeds
OUT
Claims data lake (BigQuery / Snowflake), partitioned by paid month, PHI-controlled

Eligibility / enrollment

  • member_id, date of birth, gender, ZIP / county
  • product (MA, ACA, commercial), plan, PBP
  • coverage start / end, months enrolled, gaps
  • dual / LIS, originally disabled, ESRD, institutional

Medical claims

  • service date, paid date, allowed and paid amounts
  • ICD-10-CM diagnoses (all positions), CPT / HCPCS, DRG
  • place of service, provider NPI and specialty
  • inpatient admits, length of stay, ER, observation, SNF

Pharmacy claims

  • NDC → GPI therapeutic class, generic flag
  • fill date, days supply, quantity, allowed cost
  • specialty / biologic / oncology / insulin flags
  • new starts vs refills (inputs to adherence)

CMS risk-adjustment output

  • CMS-HCC V28 RAF, current and prior year
  • the 115 payment HCCs after hierarchies
  • segment: community aged / disabled / dual, institutional, new enrollee
  • this is both a feature and the baseline to beat

Clinical / lab / HRA

  • HbA1c, eGFR, LDL, blood pressure
  • health-risk assessment answers, frailty
  • ADT admit/discharge feeds, care-management enrolment

SDOH / external

  • Area Deprivation Index, SVI at census tract
  • rurality, provider density
  • prior-plan risk score for transfers

Feature engineering · the member-year table

Turn 24 months of claims into one row per member, frozen on 31 December

This is the most important stage and the one most people skip past. Every model downstream reads one table: a row per member per year, a few hundred feature columns describing the member as of 31 Dec of year t, and a target column holding what they actually cost in year t+1. We train on past pairs where both sides are known, and score the newest row where only the features are known.

IN
Claims data lake
OUT
Feature store: member-year rows — training pairs (2023→2024), (2024→2025); locked test pair (2025→2026); scoring row (features as of Dec 2026)

Pre-processing

  • de-duplicate claims, apply adjustments and reversals
  • completion factors for the last 3 months
  • put cost on a constant-dollar basis (unit-cost trend removed)
  • annualise partial-year members: cost ÷ months × 12
  • cap the training target at $250k; keep raw cost for evaluation

Static / demographic

  • age band × gender (the 24 CMS cells)
  • product, plan, region, dual, disabled, ESRD
  • months enrolled, tenure, enrolment gaps
  • SDOH indices, rurality

Diagnosis / HCC

  • V28 HCC flags after hierarchies, counts by body system
  • chronic-condition flags and how long they've been present
  • new diagnoses this year vs persistent ones
  • cancer / cardiac / renal / transplant / dialysis flags

Utilisation (12 and 24 mo)

  • inpatient admits, LOS, ICU days, 30-day readmits
  • ER visits, observation stays, SNF and home-health days
  • outpatient surgeries, imaging, specialist visits
  • PCP visit count (a protective signal)

Cost trajectory

  • allowed cost 12 mo and 24 mo, split IP / OP / Rx / professional
  • slope: last 6 months vs prior 6
  • spike count: months above 3× the member's median
  • months since last claim; prior-year high-cost flag

Pharmacy

  • therapeutic-class counts, distinct drugs
  • specialty, oncology, biologic, insulin, anticoagulant flags
  • adherence (PDC) for chronic classes, refill gaps
  • new therapy starts — a leading indicator of next-year cost

RAF-derived the trick

  • CMS RAF for year t, year t−1, and the change
  • CMS expected cost = RAF × base rate, as a feature
  • the model then only has to learn where CMS is wrong, so it cannot do worse than CMS

Target columns

  • y = total allowed cost in year t+1
  • y_trunc = min(y, $250k) for training stability
  • y_hicc = 1 if y > $100k (and a $250k variant)
  • exposure = member-months enrolled in t+1

Sequence embeddings

  • clinical BERT over each month's diagnosis + procedure codes → PCA to 8–16 dims per month
  • or one whole-history vector as a static feature
  • check vocabulary covers ICD-10-CM / CPT / GPI first

TFT-derived features

  • monthly TFT (24 → 12) quantile forecast
  • sum of the P50 path = "sequence-model expected annual spend"
  • trajectory shape: accelerating vs decaying
  • never sum quantiles; sample paths if a P90 is needed

Leakage guard-rails
  1. Split by time, never randomly across members' overlapping windows.
  2. Nothing dated after 31 Dec of year t in the features — no 2026 diagnoses in the Dec 2025 row.
  3. Members who leave mid-year t+1: drop, or use exposure as an offset.
  4. Zero months of history is not zero cost — route to the new-enrollee model instead of imputing.
  5. HCC coding intensity rises every year; monitor it.

3
Segmentation

Route each row by how much history it has

One question decides the path: how many months of history does this member have as of 31 December? You cannot compute a cost trend for someone who joined last week, and CMS itself scores these groups separately, so using the same groups makes the comparison in stage 6 fair.

IN
Member-year rows
OUT
The same rows, tagged with a segment that selects which model scores them

Continuing members ≥ 12 mo · ~85–90%

  • full feature set including the 24-month trajectory
  • sub-segments mirror CMS: community aged / disabled / dual
  • carries most of the spend and nearly all catastrophic risk

Partial history 1–11 mo · ~5–8%

  • short-window features plus a months_observed flag
  • annualised prior cost, wide uncertainty
  • shrink toward the segment mean

New enrollees 0 mo · ~5–10%

  • demographics, dual, disabled, product, geography only
  • prior-plan RAF if it transfers
  • re-score at day 90 once first Rx and diagnoses arrive

Special populations

  • ESRD: dialysis, transplant, functioning graft
  • institutional / long-term care, hospice
  • separate calibration or fixed CMS factors

Why not one model with a segment flag?

It can work. But separate models are easier to explain to actuaries and regulators, and the distribution shape and available features differ enough that separate calibration is usually needed anyway.

4
Model layer

Two questions per member: how much, and how likely is a catastrophe?

Annual health cost is three populations glued together: a spike at exactly $0, a skewed body of ordinary spenders, and a heavy tail of cancer, cardiac, dialysis and NICU cases. No single loss function handles all three well, so the architecture aims one model at each part and combines them in stage 5.

IN
Routed member-year rows
OUT
Per member: expected cost ŷ, tail probability p_hicc, tail expected cost E[y | HiCC]

A · Expected-cost model core · LightGBM, Tweedie loss

  • gradient-boosted trees, objective = tweedie, power 1.3–1.7
  • log link with log(member-months) as exposure offset
  • Tweedie = compound Poisson-Gamma: a mass at zero plus a skewed positive part, in one model
  • monotone constraints: RAF ↑, prior cost ↑, age ↑ can only push cost up
  • CMS expected cost in as a feature ⇒ learns the residual over CMS
  • hyper-parameters via Optuna; 5-fold CV grouped by member; early stopping
  • alternative: two-part hurdle, P(y > 0) × E[y | y > 0]
  • SHAP values stored per member for explanations

B · High-cost claimant classifier

  • targets: P(y > $100k) and P(y > $250k) — roughly the top 1% and 0.2%
  • gradient boosting with class weights or minority oversampling
  • strongest signals: prior-year cost, oncology / dialysis / transplant codes, specialty Rx starts, admits, RAF change
  • published results on large commercial data: AUC ≈ 0.87–0.91 at $250k
  • used for tail correction in stage 5 and for care-management lists

C · New-enrollee / partial model

  • Tweedie GBM or GLM on demographics, SDOH, product
  • add first-90-day Rx and diagnoses when available, then re-score
  • benchmark: the CMS new-enrollee demographic model

D · Zero-spend propensity

  • P(y = 0): young, no Rx, no PCP visit
  • fixes CMS's 62% over-prediction of the healthiest quintile
  • feeds the hurdle variant and the low-risk tier

E · Temporal Fusion Transformer

  • member-month panel: 24-month encoder → 12-month decoder
  • static: age band, gender, plan, segment · known future: month, plan-year position · observed past: cost, admits, ER, chronic count, Rx, new dx
  • quantile loss; outputs monthly P10/P50/P90, variable importance, attention over time
  • role: Σ P50 becomes a feature for A, or an ensemble member — not the primary annual scorer

Why not just the TFT?

Monthly member cost is mostly zeros with rare spikes, so a monthly forecaster spends its capacity on timing noise. The business asks for one annual number, and the sum over 12 months is far smoother than any single month. Use TFT for what it adds — trajectory shape and monthly cash-flow views — after the annual backbone exists.

5
Ensemble → calibration → risk score

Combine, correct the totals, then divide by the population mean

Three short steps turn model outputs into the number the business asked for. The score sits on the same scale as CMS (average member = 1.0), so the two are directly comparable, and every score decomposes into its drivers.

IN
ŷ, p_hicc, E[y | HiCC] per member, plus validation-year actuals for calibration
OUT
risk score, expected dollars, tail probability, tier, top-5 drivers — per member and rolled up by plan, PCP, county, condition

Blend and tail correction

ŷ_blend = w₁·GBM_tweedie + w₂·hurdle + w₃·Σ TFT_P50 (weights fit on the validation year, out-of-fold)

ŷ* = (1 − p_hicc) · ŷ_blend + p_hicc · E[y | HiCC]

  • segment-specific weights (continuing / partial / new)
  • a ridge meta-learner stacks the components; never fit on rows the base models saw

Calibration

  • isotonic or Platt scaling on the validation year, per segment
  • enforce Σ predicted = Σ actual within each risk decile — this is what makes predictive ratios land at 1.0
  • recalibrate annually: normalisation factor = mean(ŷ*) over the reference population
  • coding-intensity adjustment if the score is used for payment

Risk score

risk_score = ŷ* / mean(ŷ*_reference_population) → average member = 1.0

  • tiers: low < 0.5 · moderate · high > 2.0 · catastrophic when p_hicc > τ
  • confidence band from a quantile GBM or the TFT P10–P90
  • SHAP splits each score into demographics + diagnoses + utilisation + Rx + trend

Who consumes it

  • actuarial: reserves, bid support, PMPM by cohort
  • care management: outreach lists ranked by p_hicc × impactability
  • underwriting / network: risk-adjusted benchmarks by PCP and county
  • scored 1 January, refreshed quarterly as run-out completes
Member 4471's score

ŷ* = 0.94 × $18,400 + 0.06 × $140,000 = $25,700. Population mean predicted cost is $12,000, so risk score = 2.14 → tier high. CMS gave her 1.85; the model is saying CMS under-rates her because it cannot see the rising trajectory or the new drug start.

What "better than CMS" means

CMS-HCC explains about 12% of individual cost variation (R² ≈ 0.12; ≈ 0.18 if prior-year cost is added). It over-predicts the healthiest 20% by 62% and under-predicts the top 1% by 29%. The win condition is R² in the high teens, every decile within ±5%, and clearly more of the top-1% spend captured — on the same segments, so the comparison is accepted.

6
Evaluation · champion vs challenger

Prove it on a year the model never saw, with the tests actuaries already use

Both scores — CMS-HCC and yours — are run on the same members for a held-out year, and compared on the metrics risk-adjustment reviewers expect. A decision rule agreed before looking at the results keeps the promotion honest.

IN
Scores from stage 5 for the locked test pair (features Dec 2025 → actual 2026 cost) and the CMS-HCC score for the same rows
OUT
Validation report, model card, promote / reject decision

Temporal validation design

  • train: 2022→2023 and 2023→2024
  • validate: 2024→2025 (blend weights, calibration, early stopping)
  • test, locked: 2025→2026, scored once
  • baselines: CMS-HCC V28, prior-year cost, GLM

Accuracy

  • R² on raw and on capped cost — report both
  • MAE on individuals (will look poor for every model; expected)
  • aggregate error by plan, county, PCP panel — the number finance cares about

Predictive ratios

  • PR = Σ predicted ÷ Σ actual; target 1.0 ± 5%
  • by decile of predicted risk
  • by condition: diabetes, CHF, COPD, CKD, cancer, behavioural health, no-HCC
  • by segment: dual, disabled, new enrollee, age band

Tail and classification

  • cumulative gain: share of spend captured in the top 1 / 5 / 10%
  • AUC and PR-AUC for the $100k and $250k targets
  • precision@k for the care-management list size

Fairness and stability

  • predictive-ratio parity across gender, age, dual, rural, ADI quintile
  • year-over-year score drift and rank correlation
  • SHAP importances reviewed for clinical face validity

Decision rule (agreed in advance)

Promote the challenger only if, on the locked test year: R² beats CMS by ≥ 0.04, every decile's predictive ratio is within ±5%, top-1% PR ≥ 0.90, and no fairness slice gets worse. Otherwise ship the simplest model that clears the bar.

A common mistake

Comparing on a random 20% hold-out. Rows from the same member in different years leak into each other, and the metrics come out flattering. Only a whole held-out year counts.

Deployment, monitoring, governance

Run it every January, watch it every month

Once promoted, the whole chain is a scheduled pipeline. The one thing that quietly breaks a risk model in production is drift: coding intensity creeps up, the Rx mix shifts, unit costs change. Monitoring closes the loop back to stage 2.

IN
Registered champion model, feature definitions, new claims each month
OUT
Member scores on 1 January, quarterly refreshes, drift alerts, audit trail

Pipelines

  • Vertex AI Pipelines: ingest → features → train → evaluate → register
  • dbt on BigQuery for feature SQL; Feast for point-in-time joins
  • annual full retrain in January; quarterly re-score with fresh run-out
  • model registry records version, data snapshot hash, metrics

Scoring and delivery

  • batch scoring on 1 January (Cloud Run / Dataflow) for the full membership
  • results to a BigQuery mart → Looker dashboards, actuarial extracts
  • optional online endpoint to re-score new enrollees at day 90
  • SHAP explanations stored per member for audit and care teams

Monitoring

  • feature drift (PSI) on HCC prevalence, Rx mix, cost trend
  • score drift: mean score must stay ≈ 1.0 after normalisation
  • monthly actual-vs-expected as the year emerges (rolling PR)
  • alert when top-decile PR or HiCC precision degrades

Governance

  • model-risk-management validation report and model card
  • HIPAA / PHI minimisation, access logging
  • if used for payment: RADV-style diagnosis substantiation
  • bias review, actuarial sign-off, change control
Build order

① member-year table + CMS baseline metrics, so you know the number to beat. ② Tweedie GBM (model A) — likely already beats CMS. ③ HiCC classifier (B), new-enrollee model (C), calibration. ④ only if tail predictive ratios still lag: TFT and embedding features (E).

Feedback loop

A drift alert does not mean "retrain the same model on new data". It means go back to stage 2, check whether a feature's meaning changed (a new coding rule, a formulary change), fix the definition, then retrain.

The whole thing in one paragraph

Raw claims become one feature row per member per year, frozen at 31 December. Rows are routed by how much history they carry. A Tweedie gradient-boosted model predicts each member's expected annual cost while a classifier predicts the probability of a catastrophic year; the two are blended, calibrated so totals reconcile by decile, and divided by the population mean to give a risk score with average 1.0. That score is proven against CMS-HCC on a locked-out year using R², predictive ratios and tail lift, and only then scheduled to run every January with drift monitoring feeding back into the feature definitions.

  1. Stage 2 (the member-year table) is where most of the effort and most of the risk lives.
  2. Stage 4 is the answer to "zeros and spikes": Tweedie for the body, a classifier for the tail.
  3. Stage 6 is where "better than CMS" is decided — by predictive ratios, not by MAE.
  4. TFT and embeddings are stage-2 features you add after the backbone works, not the backbone.
